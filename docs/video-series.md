# Momo shorts: the character series

Status: **proposal plus shot lists.** The six films are written out to shot level here, and each film has a spoken script. Nothing is finished: film 5 (*Make a wish*) has a first 30-second pass in progress, and one locked character sheet exists. This replaces the old idea of one generic promo film, and the old slideshow stays retired.

## What these films are for

The app does one thing: someone makes a wish, other people tap "I'm in", the gang fills up, and a host confirms a real place, time and full cost. That arc — *wish → gang → table* — is the whole product, and it is the whole story of every film.

The films sell the arc, not the interface.

- No film shows a real, dated Momo meetup. There is no confirmed public venue, named host or fixed cost yet, so every on-screen plan stays generic and every post built from these films is labelled a concept or preview.
- No invented testimonials, no fake member counts, no "join 4,000 others", no implied safety guarantees.
- Phone screens are props, not demos. Put real UI text in during the edit, never in the generated shot — AI renders garbled text, and a garbled product screen is worse than no product screen.
- Every character is already fine. Momo adds more fun and more people, it does not fix a defect.

## The idea in one line

There are six short films, each 30–45 seconds long, with six everyday people. Each one has a small weekend problem, finds Momo, and ends up at the same table with the same gang. Every film ends on the same frame: the whole gang laughing over food, with the line *"Make a wish. We'll get your gang ready."*

Why a series instead of one ad:

- Each film speaks to one kind of weekend (garba, office, sleep-in, football, trying something new, sport). People share the one that feels like *them* or their friend.
- The same gang shows up in every film. By the third film, viewers know them. That's how a friend group feels.
- As real members join, the series can grow (see "Your own episode" below).

The motto behind all of it: **screens are everywhere and making friends as an adult is weirdly hard. Momo is the excuse to just go out, have fun and find your people.** Reddit has the conversations, but nobody actually meets. Momo exists so the meetup actually happens.

## Look & feel

- **3D animated feature-film look, always.** Every character in every frame is animated. No live action, no stock footage, no camera pans over a still image, no photo cutouts. Soft, rounded characters with big expressive eyes, warm skin with subsurface light, and golden-hour colour. It should feel like a family animated movie you'd watch on a Sunday.
- **Indian and specific.** Gujarat and Ahmedabad details: chaniya choli, steel tiffin, Activa scooter, pressure-cooker whistle, cutting chai, a Manek Chowk-style night food street, a society badminton court, Law Garden ground, the Sabarmati riverside at dawn.
- **Filmy nostalgia, not parody.** Small nods everyone gets without needing film knowledge: slow-motion entrances, arms-wide hero pose, the dramatic hair flip, silent-comedy physical gags (fighting a printer, losing a fight with a bedsheet). Use *poses and moods*, never actual actors' likenesses, logos or copyrighted characters.
- **Recurring mascot:** a tiny steamed momo with a curl of steam on its head appears in every film. It peeks out of a phone screen, rides a scooter mirror, or winks from a plate. Named **Bhaap** (see Decisions below).

## Rules for every prompt

Paste these into every generation, image or video, because each one has already bitten us once:

1. Describe the style ("3D animated feature film, soft subsurface skin, expressive eyes, warm cinematic lighting"). Never type a studio or franchise name — several tools block them, and a public brand shouldn't depend on another studio's identity.
2. No brand marks anywhere, including on shoes, jerseys, bags and phone backs. Write "plain white sneakers, no logos" explicitly; models add a swoosh if you don't.
3. No readable text inside the shot. Screens, banners, calendars and shop signs stay blank or abstract; text goes in during the edit.
4. Spoken lines are seven words or fewer. Longer lines make the model rush the accent.
5. Say what the character *does*, never what they *are*. No "shy", "lonely", "awkward", "lazy", "boring", "addicted", "introvert". Situations, not diagnoses.
6. Hands and props are the usual failure point. One prop per shot, and hold it in the prompt ("holds two decorated dandiya sticks", "one hand holds a steel chai flask").
7. Reuse the locked character sheet as a reference image on every single shot of that film. Never re-describe a face from memory.
8. **Never show a phone screen face-on.** Writing "no readable text, no brand cues" does not stop the model — it rendered an iPhone running iOS Messages, notch and blue bubbles included, on a prompt that explicitly forbade both. Instead keep the screen turned away: show the back or edge of the phone, or only the glow on the character's face, or the character's reflection in dark glass. If a screen must face camera, generate the shot with the screen black and composite the UI in the edit.

## Sound & voice

This is the part the old draft was missing, and it is half the feeling of these films.

**The one rule: unhurried.** These are weekend films, not launch hype. Everything is delivered slowly, with air between the lines.

- **Narrator.** One voice across all six films, so the series sounds like one world. Proposal: a warm mid-low male voice, late twenties, Ahmedabad Hindi accent, about 90–100 words per minute, with real breaths and pauses. Friendly, like a mate telling you about his Sunday. Never an ad announcer, never a "exciting news" lift at the end line.
- **Characters.** They speak diegetically in their own voices, in short lines. Mom's call in *Sleeping Pari*, the two-word replies in *Make a wish*, the "UNO!" in *Weekend refresh*. That's it. No monologues.
- **Language.** Hinglish base, with Gujarati sprinkles in Dhwani's and Pari's films. English only for the product phrase "make a wish" and the closing line.
- **Pace budget.** Maximum six spoken lines in a 30-second film, and at least two seconds of silence before the closing line. Slow voice is a mixing decision as much as a performance one.
- **Subtitles.** Burned in, always, in the central crop-safe area. Most Reels and Shorts start muted, so every film has to read fully with sound off, then reward sound-on.
- **Music.** Soft plucked strings, light hand percussion, warm pads. Understated, building only at the moment the gang forms. Original or licensed only — no recognisable film songs, no "sounds like A.R. Rahman" in a prompt.
- **Sound design carries the city.** Ceiling fan, pressure-cooker whistle, distant traffic, dhol from a ground, scooter idle, birds on a hill at dawn, chair scrape, card shuffle. Cheap, and it does more for Ahmedabad-ness than any landmark.
- **Honest caveat about generated audio.** Video models rush Indian dialogue and flatten the accent. If the delivery comes out fast or neutral, mute the generated track and re-record the voice in ElevenLabs or on a phone in a cupboard, then lay it under the shot in CapCut. The picture is the expensive part; the voice is a five-minute fix.

## Decisions

The old draft left five questions open. Here are the answers, written as decisions so production can start. Each is reversible, and the cost of reversing goes up the further a film gets.

1. **Names stay:** Dhwani, Rohan, Pari, Gurpreet "Gary", Sameer, Meera. Locking a name is cheap to change now and expensive after a character sheet exists, so we lock before generating. Alternatives (Kavya, Jay, Tanvi, Harsh, Aditi, Kabir) move to the "Your episode" roster, where real members pick their own.
2. **The mascot is Bhaap** ("steam"). It's a Gujarati and Hindi word, it's a nickname, and it matches the steamed-momo icon the site already ships. "Chhotu" is a generic little-brother term that nothing owns, and calling it "Momo" collides with the brand name and makes search awkward.
3. **One language cut: Hinglish with Gujarati sprinkles.** A separate full Gujarati cut doubles voice, subtitle and review work for identical pictures. If demand shows up after the first three films, the Gujarati pass is a re-voice, not a re-render.
4. **The menu edition gets its own cameo, not its own mascot.** In menu-edition films, Bhaap appears alongside a cutting-chai glass with a curl of steam. The films stay food-neutral so the two editions don't compete for the same ending frame.
5. **Tool order: test the single-shot path first, then Flow/Veo for hero shots, then Kling for volume.** One 30-second reference-based render tells us what actually breaks — character drift, hands, accent — before we buy a month of anything. Quality-first films use Veo 3.1 in Flow; the films that are mostly gags can be cut on Kling for cost.

## 1. Dhwani — *Garba ke baad*

## 7. Your episode (as the community grows)

When a real member has been to a few meetups, they can *opt in* to their own 15-second episode:

1. The member picks a hobby or wish (or their story from a real meetup).
2. They can choose a **food avatar** (no photo needed) or give explicit consent to be stylised from a photo. Photo consent must be written, revocable, and deleted on request. Never use someone's likeness without consent, and never for anyone under 18.
3. We generate a character sheet in the same style, place them in a template episode, and they cameo with the default gang.
4. The member approves the final film before anything is posted.

**The template is one of the six shot lists above with the lead character swapped.** Same beats, same ending frame, same closing line. The member's name, wish and hobby fill three slots; nothing else changes. That is why the six films are written to a shared shot structure.

## Feasibility, honestly

- **What AI does well now:** 5–10 second shots with a consistent character when you supply reference sheets, warm lighting, simple actions, and short lines of dialogue.
- **What still breaks:** the same character drifting between shots (hair, clothes, jewellery), hands holding things (dandiya sticks, racquets, phones), readable text on screens, and exact Indian outfits and props. Plan for **3–6 generations per usable shot**.
- **A single 30-second generation is a scout, not a film.** One render asked to cover five locations and six people will drift the character and rush the voice. Its value is telling us *where* it breaks. The deliverable is still the per-shot path: lock the sheet, generate 6–8 shots, cut them together.
- **Workflow that works:** (1) lock each character sheet first, (2) take the shot list for that film, (3) generate each shot with the same references, (4) put phone-screen UI text in during the edit rather than in the AI shot, (5) add voices and music in the edit.
- **Effort:** roughly 1–2 days per 35-second film for one person once the sheets are locked. Do film 5 (*Make a wish*) first, because it explains the product.
- **Cost:** a paid Flow/Veo plan or a Kling plan for a month should cover a first batch of three films. Check current credit pricing before buying; prices change often.
- **Rights:** use only original characters; no real actors, franchise characters, club logos or brand logos in frame. Keep the prompts and generation records for each shot. User-supplied reference images stay unverified until their rights are confirmed — the site's own hand-drawn food art in `app/food-art.ts` is the safe reference.

### Measured motion, and what it means

`ffmpeg-static` (installed to a scratch temp dir, not the repo) gives an objective check instead of trusting the thumbnail:

```
ffmpeg -i clip.mp4 -an -vf "tblend=all_mode=difference,signalstats,metadata=print:file=-" -f null -
```

Mean per-frame luma difference on a 0–256 scale: **shot 01 = 2.1, shot 03 = 0.9, shot 04 = 2.8, shot 05 = 2.4, shot 06 = 5.9.** Every one of those short shots holds its framing from first second to last — no push-in, no tap, no mascot, no light pulses. **The single 30-second generation, by contrast, delivered the whole film:** mean 3.3 with a peak of 63 at the scene cuts, and sampled frames at 1, 13, 18 and 29 s confirm the bedroom, Bhaap appearing on the phone with Sameer wide-eyed, the sunrise ridge with four new friends and the chai flask, then the group sitting on a stone ledge eating momos.

**This inverts the workflow assumption this doc was built on.** The feasibility section says to lock a sheet and generate six to eight short shots. On this model, short per-shot generations produce beautiful frozen frames, while one long generation carrying a described five-beat arc produces real scene changes, real character reactions, and wardrobe continuity across four locations. Film 5 has a first cut, and it came from the long path — `docs/video-assets/momo-film-05-make-a-wish-30s.mp4`.

**A warning about the metric itself.** Shot 06 scored 5.9 — nearly triple the bedroom shots — and that number was misleading. Its frames at 1 s and 5 s hold identical poses and framing, so nothing was actually moving more; the scene simply has far more high-frequency texture (rock, foliage, mist, sun glare) plus grain, and compression noise on busy texture inflates per-frame difference. Read the metric only as a screening filter for "is this a frozen still", never as proof of motion. **The frame comparison is the real test**, and it is also what caught the 30-second film genuinely cutting between scenes.

**What the long path still gets wrong, in priority order:**

1. The phone at 13 s is an iPhone Pro triple-lens module. Brand likeness, must go — repaint in the edit or re-render that beat with the screen and back both hidden.
2. The ending is four people on a ledge, not the six-person gang table with the empty chair facing camera. The series' signature ending frame did not survive, and there is no clean bottom space for a wordmark.
3. The voice has not been heard. Audio is present at a sane level, mean −20.1 dB, peak −3.1 dB, but whether it is slow, whether the accent reads Ahmedabad, and whether the closing line is even there — that needs a human ear.
4. Sameer still trends worried in the opening beat, which is the register problem the short shots had too.

**Next step for the series is therefore not the per-shot path.** It is: one long generation per film, each with its beats written as an arc, and the shot lists above repurposed as the beat descriptions inside that prompt — plus the locked sheet as reference. Film 5's 30-second cut then gets the phone fixed, the ending reshot as its own long generation, and the narrator recorded once and laid over the top in the edit.

### What the first pass taught us

- **Check frames, don't trust prompts.** Shot 4 came back on-model and clean but missing its whole point — the mascot. Extracting stills at 1 s and 4 s with ffmpeg is the only reliable review step, and it also exposes shots that are secretly static. Every accepted clip should get this check before assembly.
- **Long beats beat short shots on this model.** The five-second single-action prompts produced frozen frames; the thirty-second prompt that described a five-beat arc produced real cuts, reactions and continuity. Write the arc, don't chop it up. (This reversed the assumption in the feasibility section, and the shot lists above should now be read as beat descriptions to fold into one long prompt rather than as separate generations.)
- **Name the phone.** The model rendered a triple-camera bump that reads as a specific real product. Add "plain slim dark phone, single small camera bump, no brand design cues" to every prompt that shows a phone.
- **Run a few generations at a time.** Eight parallel submissions drained capacity: the two failures were storage timeouts on the provider side, and resubmissions were refused with HTTP 403 while jobs were still in flight. Batch in groups of three.

**Narrator handling for the assembled master.** The eight shots request diegetic sound and only the short character lines, deliberately. A narrator read on eight separate generations produces eight different voices and an inconsistent tempo, which is the fastest way to make a series feel cheap. The narrator's two lines in this film — *"Sameer ne ek wish ki."* over shot 04 and *"Make a wish. We'll get your gang ready."* over shot 08 — go in during assembly from one recording, kept at 90–100 words per minute with a real pause before the closing line.
