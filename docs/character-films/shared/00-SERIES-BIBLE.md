## Shared storytelling grammar

1. Start with an observable action within the first few seconds: a dance beat, a print job, a sleepy hand finding the mattress, a boot/ball contact, a curious saved idea or a practice rhythm. An explanatory narrator is unnecessary.
2. Give the character a small desire beyond a product demonstration. They do something before and after touching a phone.
3. Let hesitation be personal and brief. Do not use shame, rejection montages that dehumanize friends, false urgency, magical notifications or a guaranteed crowd as persuasion.
4. Make a plan legible before departure. The story may omit routine form fields, but cannot pretend that unconfirmed interest equals an actual place or verified attendance.
5. Show the consequence in the body: placing a phone, shifting weight, packing a bag, leaving a chair, fastening shoes, stepping toward a table. A delighted face alone is not the whole scene.
6. Earn connection through a specific small shared action: passing a plate, making room, laughing at the same game mistake, noticing a water bottle or attending an invited performance. Do not replace that with a stock crowd cheering a brand.
7. Finish with a memory, a voluntary next step or a little joke, rather than a promise of lifelong friendship.

The default long-film mode uses character dialogue, sound and visual acting. Any older short-cut narrator is an optional future version, not a voice that must be forced over these scripts. Keep the original full performances editable so a short cut can be made without speeding everyone up.

Every episode stands alone. An unnamed supporting friend in an early scene is not automatically one of the other five leads. A recurring cameo needs its reference, wardrobe and voice assignment before it enters a frame. Do not silently ask one generation to invent all six leads around one table.

The shared-table idea can recur as a composition: warm food, ordinary chairs, someone making room. It is a visual motif, not a requirement that the identical six adults attend every fictional outing. ## Setting and practical story logic

The new long versions use urban Ahmedabad-inspired homes, offices, garba grounds, a football turf, public food/café settings, a riverside walk, a badminton court and a small performance space. These are fictional story settings until actual logistics are confirmed.

- No mountain ridge, alpine mist or remote sunrise trek is presented as an Ahmedabad local outing.
- A night food stop needs a later confirmed place with operating hours and practical transport. Do not assert a named business is open at 1 a.m. merely because the scene is after garba.
- A riverside meeting point, date, gate/access arrangements, route, host, end time and cost are placeholders. A story of a walk is not an operationally approved public event.
- Friday office, Saturday leisure, Sunday match, afternoon invitation and multi-week friendship sequences need explicit visual/time transitions; a door cannot magically turn Friday night into Saturday morning without an edited jump.
- Athletic play, costume preparation and dance performance are different environments with tracked footwear, hair, props and sound.

## Product and invitation integrity

Use the visible brand **Momo to Pizza**; characters may naturally call it Momo. Both food editions remain. The film is a story, not a screen recording or a real testimonial.

Phone screens are preferably turned away or replaced with a controlled blank-screen plate for readable edited UI. A plain unbranded device has no recognizable logo, operating-system messages, triple-camera styling or copied app interface. Keep hands/phone singular and in contact.

Plans in scripts should include readable confirmed details in the final edited insert: date, public place/city, named host, start/end time, full cost and what is included. Until those exist, use bracketed production placeholders and label the piece a fictional concept. Do not fabricate member counts, live events, social proof, prices or safety claims.

The pre-meetup planning chat is in the site. An optional after-meetup group is a separate concept, restricted to actual attendees who choose an invitation. Interest is not attendance. A regular phone-number-based group needs clear contact-visibility disclosure. No script authorizes sending real email, messaging real recipients or enabling a delivery feature.

The older line “We'll get your gang ready” is not mandatory in these long films because it can imply a guaranteed result. A quiet working closing thought such as “Ek chhota sa plan. Baaki, milkar.” or the film's own payoff can be proposed for review. No new tagline is declared an approved product commitment.

## Sound, language and performance

Hinglish is conversational. Gujarati, Punjabi or another language cue belongs only where natural to that individual character, not as an exaggerated accent or a joke. Every lead has their own voice brief and speaking rhythm. Do not cast six versions of the same voice merely by altering pitch.

Room tone and object sounds carry place: fan, cloth, steel cup, laptop hinge, chair, footstep, ball, dhol fading after a ground, a racquet contact or stage ambience. They must follow visible action. Use original or properly cleared music; no named-film melody or celebrity soundalike is specified.

Voice is prepared separately, by line ID, before final mouth timing. A breath, look, gesture and silence can do more than another explanation. Short lines need room before and after them. Slower or faster emotion is a performance note, not permission to time-stretch words unnaturally.
