# Meera — full treatment and screenplay

**A Seat in the Audience · 160-second working cut · 32 shots, six editable batches.** The story spans several weekends and a later performance day. No real court booking, public performance, attendee list or automatic notification is represented as having happened.

## Logline and emotional movement

A 24-year-old Bharatanatyam practitioner asks for badminton partners, discovers the ease of a repeated Sunday plan, and gradually feels comfortable inviting those people to her performance. Their quiet presence in the audience becomes a shared memory over tea.

The first want is practical: another person on the court. The deeper move is sharing something that matters beyond the game. The obstacle is neither a villain nor a broken personality; it is the ordinary effort of making a plan, agreeing details, meeting again and offering a more personal invitation without demanding a yes. The film does not show an app manufacturing best friends. It shows the choices and repetitions that can make a new connection possible.

Meera has a life before and after the screen. She practises, has responsibilities not exhaustively specified here, misses a shuttle, laughs, thinks and prepares for a performance. The quiet audience recognition matters because we saw the friends as ordinary partners before we saw them as an audience. Keep those layers. Do not replace the dance payoff with a sports tournament simply because a smash looks dramatic.

Supporting cast is limited to two original fictional adults: **Nisha**, the first person to respond and later an honest interested listener, and **Aarav**, another court companion who carries a small chai joke and the after-performance appreciation. They need stable original faces and clothes, not elaborate new biographies or a likeness of a real member. The film does not depend on every other lead appearing in its auditorium.

Never publish the brackets or replace them with invented actual event claims. The folded programme can remain blank in the motion take. Readable text, subtitles and group/wish actions belong in a separately composed insert so they can be corrected without remaking facial acting.

## Protect these details in shorter or longer revisions

A shorter cut may combine the second/third-Sunday rally and towel details, but it still needs a clear repeated-meetings cue before the personal invitation. A longer cut can let a first greeting breathe, show another rally or hold the performance excerpt longer. Neither version should remove the dance payoff and quietly restore the old tournament-only ending. Do not pad runtime with a forced full-cast parade or identical silent close-ups.

The story does not require Tara's email glow, cap joke, panda or attendees-only group. Those belong to her episode. If a later chat/group action is added, it must be voluntary and must not imply unsupported automation. Meera's own recurring motif is **rhythm**: practice steps, a rally, cups placed down, another week, then her friends' applause after the phrase has ended.
